## SECTION 1 — SOURCE TEXT  (paste this as a NotebookLM source)

**How big is space? Measuring the universe with AU, light-years and parsecs**

Space is so big that our everyday units, like kilometres, quickly become useless. If you tried to write the distance to another galaxy in kilometres, you would need a number with more than twenty digits. So astronomers use three special "rulers" instead: the astronomical unit, the light-year, and the parsec.

The first ruler is the **astronomical unit**, written **AU**. One AU is the distance between the Earth and the Sun: about 150 million kilometres. Inside our Solar System this is handy. The Earth is 1 AU from the Sun. Mars is about 1.5 AU. Jupiter is about 5 AU, and Neptune, the farthest planet, is about 30 AU. Pluto, out at the edge, is about 39 AU from the Sun. Sunlight, moving at the speed of light, takes 8 minutes to travel that 1 AU from the Sun to the Earth.

That speed of light is important. Light is the fastest thing in the universe: it travels about 300,000 kilometres every single second. Nothing can go faster.

The second ruler uses that speed. A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one whole year. It is not a unit of time, even though the word contains "year" — it is a distance. One light-year is about 9.46 trillion kilometres. That is a 9 with twelve zeros after it. The nearest star to our Sun, called Proxima Centauri, is 4.24 light-years away. This means the light you see from that star tonight actually left it more than four years ago. When you look far out into space, you are always looking back in time.

The third ruler is the one professional astronomers use most: the **parsec**. One parsec is about 3.26 light-years. For bigger distances they use the **kiloparsec**, which is one thousand parsecs, and the **megaparsec**, which is one million parsecs. Our galaxy, the Milky Way, is about 30 kiloparsecs across — that is roughly 100,000 light-years from one side to the other.

Let us put it all together to feel the scale. Light crosses one AU, from the Sun to the Earth, in 8 minutes. To cross to the nearest star takes light more than 4 years. To cross our whole galaxy takes light 100,000 years. And the observable universe — everything we can possibly see — is about 93 billion light-years across. Beyond its edge, the universe may be far bigger still.

So next time you look up at night, remember: the tiny points of light are unimaginably far away, and you are seeing them not as they are now, but as they were long, long ago.

## SECTION 2 — AUDIO PROMPT  (paste into "Customize" before generating)

Create a short, friendly conversation between the two hosts for teenage English learners (about 14–15 years old, CEFR level A2–B1). Speak clearly and a little slowly, using simple, everyday words and short sentences. Keep it to about 4 to 5 minutes.

Focus only on the scale of space and these key terms, and say each one clearly: astronomical unit (AU), the speed of light, light-year, parsec, kiloparsec, megaparsec. Explain that a light-year is a distance and NOT a time. Repeat the most important numbers slowly and clearly: 1 AU = 150 million km; light = 300,000 km per second; sunlight takes 8 minutes to reach Earth; Proxima Centauri is 4.24 light-years away; 1 parsec = 3.26 light-years; the Milky Way is about 100,000 light-years wide; the observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across. End with the idea that looking into space means looking back in time. Avoid difficult vocabulary and long tangents.

## SECTION 3 — LISTENING QUESTIONS  (students answer while / after listening)

### A. Before you listen (warm-up, speaking)
1. How do you think scientists measure the distance to a star? Why can't they just use kilometres?
2. Guess: how long does light from the Sun take to reach the Earth?

### B. While you listen — True or False
1. A light-year measures **time**, not distance.  → **( F )**
2. Light travels about 300,000 km per second.  → **( T )**
3. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach the Earth.  → **( T )**
4. Proxima Centauri is the farthest star from the Sun.  → **( F — it is the nearest )**
5. One parsec is smaller than one light-year.  → **( F — 1 parsec ≈ 3.26 light-years )**

### C. While you listen — Fill in the number
1. 1 AU (Earth–Sun) ≈ __________ million km.  → **150**
2. Light travels about __________ km every second.  → **300,000**
3. Proxima Centauri is __________ light-years away.  → **4.24**
4. 1 parsec ≈ __________ light-years.  → **3.26**
5. The Milky Way is about __________ light-years across.  → **100,000**
6. The observable universe is about __________ billion light-years across.  → **93**

### D. While you listen — Multiple choice
1. A **light-year** is a unit of…
   a) time  b) **distance**  c) brightness  d) speed
2. Which ruler do professional astronomers use most for big distances?
   a) the kilometre  b) the astronomical unit  c) **the parsec**  d) the mile
3. A **kiloparsec** is…
   a) **1,000 parsecs**  b) 100 parsecs  c) 1 million parsecs  d) 3.26 light-years
4. "Looking far into space means looking back in ________."
   a) space  b) **time**  c) light  d) distance

### E. After you listen — Short answer / speaking
1. In your own words, explain **why** a light-year is a distance and not a time.
2. Why do we see stars "as they were in the past" and not as they are now?
3. Put these in order from **smallest to largest** distance: *parsec · light-year · astronomical unit · kiloparsec*.
   → **AU < light-year < parsec < kiloparsec**
4. Discuss: does thinking about these huge distances make you feel small, curious, or something else? Explain in 3–4 English sentences.
